Why does a toilet stop filling by itself?

Nobody turns it off. Press the handle and watch what does.

Fig. 02 — cistern, cut awayFront view · NTS
Water used0.0 L6 L is one flush
1The handle lifts the flush valve, a plug with a rubber seal in the bottom of the cistern.
2Water rushes into the bowl. The float drops with it, and that opens the fill valve.
3Fresh water pours in. As the float rises, its arm pushes the fill valve shut.
!The float can't rise, so the fill valve never shuts. Extra water spills down the overflow pipe into the bowl and runs away for ever. That's a "running toilet".

One float does all the thinking

No electricity, no computer. Just a float, a lever and a spare way out.

FIG 2.1 · FLOAT

Why it floats

The float is hollow, so it's mostly air. The water pushes it up harder than its weight pulls it down, so it rides on the surface.

FIG 2.2 · LEVER

A lever makes it strong

The float pushes gently, but it sits on a long arm. Near the pivot, that gentle push becomes strong enough to hold back the water from the mains.

FIG 2.3 · BACK-UP

A spare way out

The overflow pipe stands 20 mm above the water line. If the float ever fails, the extra water runs into the bowl instead of flooding the bathroom.

The big idea: a float is a switch the water flips by itself. When the water is low, the float lets more in. When it's full, the float shuts it off. Nobody has to watch it.
