Fig. 10 · The rocket

How does a rocket fly with nothing to push on?

In space there's no air and no ground. Pull a rocket apart, look inside its motor, then fire it up and see what it throws out of the back.

Throw gas down, go up

What happens inside a rocket motor, every second it fires.

FIG 10.1 · PUMP

Shove it in

A turbopump as powerful as 55 F1 cars spins 5,500 times a minute, forcing fuel and liquid oxygen into the engine: about 2.6 tonnes every second.

FIG 10.2 · BURN

Burn it hot

The injector sprays them together through over 6,000 tiny holes. They burn at about 3,300 °C and make a huge amount of hot gas.

FIG 10.3 · SQUEEZE

Squeeze it out

The gas can only escape through the narrow throat. Then the bell-shaped nozzle lets it spread out and speed up to about 2.6 km every second.

FIG 10.4 · PUSH

Push back

Throwing all that gas down pushes the rocket up. It doesn't need air or ground to push on, which is why rockets work in space.

The big idea: a rocket is mostly tanks. Nearly 90% of a Saturn V's weight at lift-off was fuel and liquid oxygen, and the motor's whole job is to throw that out of the back as fast as it can.
